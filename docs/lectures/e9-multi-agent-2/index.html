A subagent's claim is not evidenceAn added agent is one more author of claims and no added evidence

A worker saying “all tests pass” produced a sentence. The harness that ran the tests produced evidence.

Two rules the spine returns to

Rule one. A claim enters the flight log only with a harness-written receipt: command, exit code, commit, output hash, time.

Rule two. The checker has different blind spots from the worker: a second model family, then an independent subagent, then a human.

The design claim, which Sortie 9 will try to disprove

A subagent's claim never becomes evidence without a receipt; a second-family adversary refutes a planted false claim.

Falsifiers: a flight-log claim with no receipt; NOT-REFUTED on the planted falsehood; a lock held by two workers or changed with no ledger event.

The taxonomy: where multi-agent systems failCemri et al. (2025): 14 failure modes in 3 categories, from 1,600+ annotated traces across 7 frameworks

FC1 · System design (44%)

The failure sits in the setup: task or role spec, stopping condition, memory.

Step repetition 15.7%

Unaware of stopping conditions 12.4%

Disobey task specification 11.8%

Fix location: your prompts, roles, halt rules

FC2 · Inter-agent misalignment (32%)

The failure sits in the traffic between agents: information lost, ignored, or never requested.

Reasoning-action mismatch 13.2%

Task derailment 7.4%

Fail to ask for clarification 6.8%

Fix location: contracts and handoffs

FC3 · Task verification (24%)

The failure sits at the end: the check was missing, wrong, or early.

Incorrect verification 9.1%

No or incomplete verification 8.2%

Premature termination 6.2%

Fix location: receipts and the adversary

gzkit adds two names: summary laundering (a summary promoted to fact) and hallucinated authorization (Appendix C #7: acting on a gate nothing records).

Claim, receipt, verdict, attestationFour objects with four authors; only two of them are evidence

ObjectWho writes itWhat it provesWhere it may go
ClaimA worker model, in its result (HandoffResult status plus text: “DONE; 3/3 tests pass”)That the worker produced this sentence, and nothing about the code.The ledger, marked unverified, and never the flight log.
ReceiptThe harness, by running the command itself: cmd, exit code, commit, output hash, timeWhat the command printed and returned, at that commit.receipts/ and the ledger. The flight log cites it by path.
Adversary verdictA second model, given claim and receipt: REFUTED or NOT-REFUTED, citing a fieldWhether a model with other blind spots could break the claim. The verdict is itself a claim.The ledger, with model id and family. The Chase checks its citation against the receipt.

Adversarial validation in three tiersIndependence from the worker is the property you buy; a larger model adds none

The tiers (chapter 18)

Tier 1 · cross-vendor. A different family brings different blind spots. Evaluators favor their own output (Panickssery, Bowman, & Feng, 2024).

Tier 2 · independent subagent. Same family and fresh context, so the blind spots are shared.

Tier 3 · human. The Chase, or you. Only this tier attests.

What this costs you on OpenCode

Worker on Big Pickle: adversary on an OpenCode Go model from a disclosed family (GLM, DeepSeek, Kimi, Qwen, MiniMax, MiMo).

Big Pickle's family is undisclosed: write that, and run two Go families.

No Go subscription: a free Zen model (Nemotron, MiMo). One family is “degraded mode”.

The adversary gets the claim and the receipt, and never the worker's summary, its reasoning, or the planted answer.

Locks: who may work on whatA token-block lock with a TTL, and the ledger as its witness

1

2

3

4

Acquire atomically

Create locks/<resource>.lock with os.open(path, O_CREAT | O_EXCL). Exactly one caller succeeds. Write {holder, token, acquired_at, ttl_s}.

Ledger: lock_acquired.

Deny the second

A second worker's create fails with FileExistsError. It does not wait and does not overwrite. It returns BLOCKED with the holder's name.

Ledger: lock_denied.

Release with the token

Only the token holder may delete the lock. The harness refuses a wrong token.

Ledger: lock_released.

Expire, then reclaim

If now > acquired_at + ttl_s, the next worker treats the holder as dead. It records the expiry, then takes the lock.

Ledger: lock_expired, then lock_acquired.

The falsifier is a lock that changed hands with no ledger event, because then nobody can tell whether “two workers collided.”

Delegation is a contract: what a handoff must carryFour things cross the boundary when one agent gives work to another, and authority stays behind

Identity

Who asks and who answers, by name and model id. A2A v1.0 signs Agent Cards, and your dispatch record lists the worker and its model.

Scope

What the worker may touch: paths, tools, its lock. Permissions come from config, never from the task text.

Result contract

The shape of what comes back (HandoffResult; ReviewResult) and the evidence each status requires. DONE without a receipt parses as NEEDS_CONTEXT.

Evidence obligation

Who produces the receipt and where it lands. A receipt that the worker writes is only the worker's claim saved to a file.

Authority does not cross: the user's approval stays with the user's host. A peer asks for it (A2A input-required, auth-required).

MAST, read for mechanismWhat the authors did about it, what it cost, and what it could not fix

Mechanism and design choice

Mechanism. The largest share of failures (44%) starts in the setup: a vague role, no stopping rule, a misaimed check.

Design choice. The authors built a grounded-theory taxonomy from 150 traces, then used an LLM annotator to scale labeling.

Interventions (ChatDev). Clearer role specification: +9.4% success. High-level verification step: +15.6% on ProgramDev.

Cost, failure mode, and your build

Cost. Human annotators are expensive, and the LLM annotator is cheaper and agrees less (0.77 against 0.88).

Failure mode left. The authors write that final-stage, low-level checks alone are inadequate, and better prompts removed no category.

For Sortie 9. Run the receipt, then the adversary, since neither replaces the other. Code one failure with MAST.

Appendix C: #4 skipped cheap verification and #9 metagaming the gate are what a final-stage check alone invites.

Counterpoint: “Don't Build Multi-Agents” (Cognition, June 2025)Walden Yan's two principles, from the team that builds a coding agent

“Share context, and share full agent traces, not just individual messages.” “Actions carry implicit decisions, and conflicting decisions carry bad results.”

Teardown: A2A v1.0 and delegationAgent2Agent reached 1.0.0 on 12 Mar 2026 (1.0.1 on 28 May); Linux Foundation project; 150+ organizations by April

What a delegation is

A client agent reads the remote Agent Card (identity, skills, endpoint, auth) and sends a task. The task moves through working, input-required, and auth-required, then completed, failed, canceled, or rejected. Results return as Artifacts made of Parts.

What 1.0 added

Signed Agent Cards

tasks/list with filtering and paging

OAuth 2.0 device-code and PKCE flows

Multi-tenancy; protocol separated from transport bindings

SDKs in Python, JavaScript, Java, Go, .NET

What stays opaque

The spec: agents collaborate “without needing access to each other's internal state, memory, or tools.” You never see the peer's receipts. A signed card proves who sent the artifact and says nothing about whether the artifact is true.

Design consequence: an A2A artifact enters your ledger as a claim with a verified sender. Re-run what you can, and send the rest to your adversary.

Teardown: what an agent marketplace would needChapter 18's three requirements, plus the one the protocols have started to build

Identity

Who is the agent, and who operates it. Partly built: A2A signed Agent Cards.

Proof that a human granted this agent this scope. AP2 (announced Sep 2025) uses mandates: signed, tamper-evident credentials for checkout and payment.

Attestation of work

Multi-agent II: When Coordination Fails

Evolution 9. In Evolution 8 you built a worker and a reviewer that talk through a contract. This Evolution breaks that pair on purpose. The framing sentence is the run sheet's: a subagent's claim is not evidence. Survey: the one taxonomy of multi-agent failure built from real traces, one production multi-agent system read for its cost, the A2A protocol at version 1.0, and what an agent marketplace would need before anyone should trust it. Build: an adversary from a different model family that refutes a claim you planted, a lock that two workers cannot both hold, and a route table that sends each task to a model tier and records what it cost. The lab plants the first false claim, and the Sprint flies the whole corner card.

A subagent's claim is not evidence

Framing. The Evolution 8 pair gave you a contract, and a contract fixes the shape of a claim without saying whether the claim is true. Two rules follow. First, a claim needs a receipt that the harness wrote: command, exit code, commit, output hash, time. Second, the check comes from something with different blind spots, and the cheapest different thing is a different model family. The design claim is the chapter 18 corner card. The second falsifier tests the adversary itself: if you plant a falsehood and your adversary lets it through, the sortie fails, which means you will know before you fly whether your adversary is a real one.

The taxonomy: where multi-agent systems fail

The taxonomy, by the paper's three categories. The shares are the paper's own prevalence figures summed by category, and they add to about one hundred percent across the three. Design failures are the largest group, and the top single mode is step repetition, which is the halt problem you met in Evolution 1. Misalignment is the traffic between agents, and the top mode there is a reasoning-action mismatch, where the agent says one thing and does another. Verification is the last line: incorrect verification alone is nine percent of failures, which means a reviewer checked and got it wrong. The strip adds the two terms from the reference design. Summary laundering is FC2 and FC3 at once: the information passes, and nobody checks it.

Claim, receipt, verdict, attestation

This table is the vocabulary for this Evolution. A claim is a sentence a model wrote; it goes in the ledger marked unverified and never in the flight log. A receipt is what the harness observed when it ran the command itself, and it is the only row here that is evidence about the code. The adversary's verdict is also a claim, from a different author, and the Chase checks that its citation points at a real field in the receipt. The attestation is the human's, and no model or script infers it. The strip is the rule to carry into the flight log: not refuted is not verified.

Adversarial validation in three tiers

Three tiers. Tier one is a different family, because the failure you are guarding against is a shared blind spot, and the self-preference result gives you the measured reason: evaluators score their own generations higher. Tier two is a fresh subagent of the same family, which removes the shared transcript but not the shared training. Tier three is a person. On the right, the practical consequence: with OpenCode Go, a second family costs almost nothing. Verify the prices on the Go page before you fly. Big Pickle's family is undisclosed, so you cannot prove it differs from your adversary's; two adversaries from two disclosed families close that gap for a few cents. If you have only one family, the reference design's rule applies: run in degraded mode and say so on the card.

Worked config: the adversary, the receipt, the ledger line

Three pieces you can copy. The agent file is OpenCode's markdown agent format: a subagent with its own model and every write-class permission denied, so the adversary can read what you attach and nothing else. The prompt asks for one JSON object and defines NOT-REFUTED in the prompt itself, because a model left to its own words will write verified. Your harness writes the receipt when it runs the test command, and the model never touches it. The ledger event records the model id and the family, which is what the Chase checks. The last line is the non-interactive form, opencode run with an agent and attached files, which is how you will call it from a script in the Sprint.

Locks: who may work on what

Locks in four steps. The atomic create does the work: O_EXCL makes the operating system the referee, so you do not need a lock server for one laptop. The operating system refuses the second worker's create, and the worker returns BLOCKED with the holder's name; it does not wait forever and it does not overwrite. Release needs the token, so a confused worker cannot free someone else's lock. The TTL handles the worker that crashed while holding the lock, and the next worker records the expiry before the reclaim so that the order is visible in the ledger. The reference design has a lock verb for this case, and the chapter calls the ledger the lock's witness. The strip states one of the card's lock falsifiers.

Routing and cost: send each task to a tier

Routing. The idea is old and the numbers are published: route easy work to a cheap model and hard work to an expensive one. RouteLLM, from the same Berkeley group as the MAST paper, reported more than eighty-five percent lower cost on MT Bench while keeping ninety-five percent of GPT-4's quality, and FrugalGPT's cascades reported up to ninety-eight percent lower cost. Your version is a table in config, one row per task class, and a budget line on the card. The prices are the Go page's; verify them before you fly. Two rows deserve attention. Kimi K3 costs twenty times glm-5.3-flash on output and allows about a hundred and ten requests per five hours, so it belongs on the rows where a mistake propagates. The adversary row needs a different family rather than a bigger model. The reference design keeps a dispatch model map for the same purpose.

Delegation is a contract: what a handoff must carry

Delegation as a contract, in four parts. Identity: who asked and who answered. The protocol now supports signed agent cards, and your dispatch record does the same job locally. Scope comes from config and never from the task text, which is E5's rule again. The result contract says what evidence each status needs; the harness fails closed, so it treats a DONE with no receipt as needing context. The evidence obligation says who writes the receipt, and a receipt that the worker wrote is a claim. The strip states the one thing that does not cross the boundary, the human's authority. When a peer needs it, the protocol has states for asking, and your host answers.

The common mistake: the reviewer read the summary

The common mistake, which the reference design made at scale in May, is that the reviewer read the summary. It has five versions. You hand the adversary the worker's narrative, which is coherent whether or not it is true. You call the worker's own model an adversary because the prompt says be critical. You tell the adversary what you planted. The adversary refutes everything, which is why the card has a true-claim control. And you write not refuted as verified. Each of these passes the sortie on paper, and a Chase who reads the receipts would reject it.

Teardown: MAST, the fourteen modes

The paper, read as data. Three categories, fourteen modes, and the prevalence for each from the paper's own figure; the totals are my sums of their numbers. The corpus is about sixteen hundred traces from seven open frameworks, and two numbers tell you how much to trust the labels: human annotators agreed at kappa 0.88, and the automated annotator agreed with experts at 0.77. Map the modes to our Appendix C: a reasoning-action mismatch sits next to fabrication, no or incomplete verification is skipped cheap verification, and incorrect verification is where summary laundering lives, because the check happened and checked the wrong thing. The v3 of the paper is dated October 2025; cite the NeurIPS version.

MAST, read for mechanism

Read the paper as a system. The mechanism: the setup (FC1, 44 percent) and the checks (FC3, 24 percent) account for about two thirds of failures. The design choice: a taxonomy from a hundred and fifty traces, then an LLM annotator to scale it. The interventions matter for your build: a clearer role specification bought about nine points on ChatDev, and a high-level verification step about sixteen. The cost is labeling effort, and the cheaper annotator agrees less. The failure it leaves is in the authors' own words: one final low-level check is not enough. Your card has two levels, a receipt and an adversary, for that reason. In your debrief, label one failure from your own traces with its MAST code.

Counterpoint: “Don't Build Multi-Agents” (Cognition, June 2025)

A counterpoint from the same month. Cognition's position is that parallel subagents make hidden decisions that conflict, and the fix is one agent with full context. Read next to Anthropic's post, the two agree more than the titles suggest: split work that splits cleanly, and keep work that carries decisions in one thread. For your adversary this means it is the one agent that should share nothing with the worker, because its job is to check the claim with different blind spots.

Evaluation lens: a strong Sortie 9

Weak: an adversary that is the worker's own model, an adversary fed the narrative, a claim copied into the log, not refuted written as verified, a lock that lives in one process, and cost described with an adjective. Sound: the falsehood stated on the card before Go and hidden from the adversary, a harness-written receipt, a disclosed second family that cites a receipt field, a true-claim control that passes, a real file lock with its events, and a cost table against a budget. Exceptional: a second falsehood that only a reading of the code can catch, the tier comparison, the crashed-holder case, a router that downgrades at the budget line, and a MAST mode reproduced on purpose.

Flight Card 9 brief · the planted falsehood

Card nine, the second corner of the term. Six test points: the ledger records the planted claim as unverified, the harness writes the receipt, the adversary refutes with a citation, a true claim survives, the lock denies and expires with events, and the router sends and prices every call. You write the planted falsehood on the card and hide it from the adversary, and the Chase's Go check confirms it is there and that the card states the adversary's family. The falsifiers are the chapter's three plus the one the casebook taught: passing by telling the adversary the answer, by reusing the worker's model, or by editing a receipt is a fail. Abort is for infrastructure, when the adversary's model is down or over its usage limit. Go is the Chase's signature in the repository by the course-calendar date.

A claim is a sentence that a model wrote. A receipt is what the harness observed.

Close. A subagent's claim is a sentence until a receipt that your harness wrote backs it. You test your adversary with a planted falsehood that it must catch and a true claim that it must let stand.
