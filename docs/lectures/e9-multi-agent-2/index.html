slide 03

A subagent's claim is not evidence

Framing. The Evolution 8 pair gave you a contract, and a contract fixes the shape of a claim without saying whether the claim is true. Two rules follow. First, a claim needs a receipt that the harness wrote: command, exit code, commit, output hash. Second, the check comes from something with different blind spots, and the cheapest different thing is a different model family. The design claim is the chapter 18 corner card. The second falsifier tests the adversary itself: if you plant a falsehood and your adversary lets it through, the sortie fails, which means you will know before you fly whether your adversary is a real one.

The slide's wording in full
  • A worker that reports “all tests pass” has produced only a sentence. Evidence comes from the harness, because the harness ran the tests and recorded the exit code.
  • Rule one. A claim moves toward the flight log only with a receipt: the command, the exit code, the commit, and a hash of the output, written by the harness and never by a model. / Rule two. The check comes from something that does not share the worker's blind spots: a second model family first, an independent subagent second, a human third. A reviewer that shares the worker's model shares its mistakes.
  • A subagent's claim never becomes evidence without a receipt, and a second-family adversary refutes a false claim that you planted. Any one of these falsifies it: a claim in the flight log with no receipt; the adversary returning NOT-REFUTED on the planted falsehood; a lock held by two workers, or a lock change with no ledger event.
slide 04

The taxonomy: where multi-agent systems fail

The taxonomy, by the paper's three categories. The shares are the paper's own prevalence figures summed by category, and they add to about one hundred percent across the three. Design failures are the largest group, and the top single mode is step repetition, which is the halt problem you met in Evolution 1. Misalignment is the traffic between agents, and the top mode there is a reasoning-action mismatch, where the agent says one thing and does another. Verification is the last line: incorrect verification alone is nine percent of failures, which means a reviewer checked and got it wrong. The strip adds the two terms from the reference design. Summary laundering is FC2 and FC3 at once: the information passes, and nobody checks it.

The slide's wording in full
  • The failure is in the setup: the task or role specification, the stopping condition, the memory. / Step repetition 15.7% / Unaware of stopping conditions 12.4% / Disobey task specification 11.8%
  • The failure is at the end: the check was missing, wrong, or came too early. / Incorrect verification 9.1% / No or incomplete verification 8.2% / Premature termination 6.2%
  • The failure is in the traffic between agents: information lost, ignored, or never asked for. / Reasoning-action mismatch 13.2% / Task derailment 7.4% / Fail to ask for clarification 6.8%
  • gzkit adds two names the paper does not use: summary laundering (a summary is promoted to fact) and hallucinated authorization (Appendix C #7: acting on a gate that nothing records).
slide 05

Claim, receipt, verdict, attestation

This table is the vocabulary for this Evolution. A claim is a sentence a model wrote; it goes in the ledger marked unverified and never in the flight log. A receipt is what the harness observed when it ran the command itself, and it is the only row here that is evidence about the code. The adversary's verdict is also a claim, from a different author, and the Chase checks that its citation points at a real field in the receipt. The attestation is the human's, and no model or script infers it. The strip is the rule to carry into the flight log: not refuted is not verified.

The slide's wording in full
  • A second model, given the claim and the receipt: REFUTED or NOT-REFUTED, with the field it cites
  • That a model with other blind spots could or could not break the claim with this evidence. It is itself a claim.
  • HandoffResult statuses from Sortie 8: DONE | DONE_WITH_CONCERNS | NEEDS_CONTEXT | BLOCKED. Only a receipt turns a claim into evidence, so the adversary's verdict alone leaves the claim unverified.
slide 06

Adversarial validation in three tiers

Three tiers. Tier one is a different family, because the failure you are guarding against is a shared blind spot, and the self-preference result gives you the measured reason: evaluators score their own generations higher. Tier two is a fresh subagent of the same family, which removes the shared transcript but not the shared training. Tier three is a person. On the right, the practical consequence: with OpenCode Go, a second family costs almost nothing. Verify the prices on the Go page before you fly. Big Pickle's family is undisclosed, so you cannot prove it differs from your adversary's; two adversaries from two disclosed families close that gap for a few cents. If you have only one family, the reference design's rule applies: run in degraded mode and say so on the card.

The slide's wording in full
  • Tier 1 · cross-vendor. A different model family. It does not share the worker's training data, tokenizer, or house style, so it is least likely to find the worker's mistake plausible. Self-preference is measured: model evaluators recognize and score their own generations higher than equal work from others (Panickssery, Bowman, & Feng, 2024). / Tier 2 · independent subagent. Same family, fresh context, no access to the worker's transcript. Cheaper to arrange; shares the family's blind spots. / Tier 3 · human. The Chase, or you. Slowest; the only tier that can attest.
  • Tier 1 is cheap this term. If your worker runs on Big Pickle, run the adversary on an OpenCode Go model from a disclosed family: GLM, DeepSeek, Kimi, Qwen, MiniMax, MiMo. glm-5.3-flash lists $0.15 in and $0.50 out per million tokens; one 3,000-token adversary call costs well under a tenth of a cent. / Big Pickle is a stealth model: its family is not published. Write “worker family: undisclosed,” and run two adversaries from two Go families to close the gap. / No Go subscription: use a free Zen model from a disclosed family (Nemotron, MiMo). With one family only, say “degraded mode” on the card.
  • The adversary gets the claim and the receipt. It never gets the worker's summary, its reasoning, or the answer you planted.

slide 08

Locks: who may work on what

Locks in four steps. The atomic create does the work: O_EXCL makes the operating system the referee, so you do not need a lock server for one laptop. The operating system refuses the second worker's create, and the worker returns BLOCKED with the holder's name; it does not wait forever and it does not overwrite. Release needs the token, so a confused worker cannot free someone else's lock. The TTL handles the worker that crashed while holding the lock, and the next worker records the expiry before the reclaim so that the order is visible in the ledger. The reference design has a lock verb for this case, and the chapter calls the ledger the lock's witness. The strip states one of the card's lock falsifiers.

The slide's wording in full
  • Create locks/<resource>.lock with os.open(path, O_CREAT | O_EXCL). The operating system lets exactly one caller create it. Write {holder, token, acquired_at, ttl_s}. / Ledger: lock_acquired.
  • A second worker's create fails with FileExistsError. It does not wait silently and does not overwrite. It returns BLOCKED with the holder's name. / Ledger: lock_denied.
  • Only the caller holding the token may delete the lock. A release with the wrong token is refused. / Ledger: lock_released.
  • If now > acquired_at + ttl_s, the holder is presumed dead. The next worker records the expiry before it takes the lock. / Ledger: lock_expired, then lock_acquired.
  • The falsifier is a lock that changed hands with no ledger event. A collision that the ledger records can be traced; without the event, nobody can tell whether two workers collided.
slide 09

Routing and cost: send each task to a tier

Routing. The idea is old and the numbers are published: route easy work to a cheap model and hard work to an expensive one. RouteLLM, from the same Berkeley group as the MAST paper, reported more than eighty-five percent lower cost on MT Bench while keeping ninety-five percent of GPT-4's quality, and FrugalGPT's cascades reported up to ninety-eight percent lower cost. Your version is a table in config, one row per task class, and a budget line on the card. The prices are the Go page's; verify them before you fly. Two rows deserve attention. Kimi K3 costs twenty times glm-5.3-flash on output and allows about a hundred and ten requests per five hours, so it belongs on the rows where a mistake propagates. The adversary row needs a different family rather than a bigger model. The reference design keeps a dispatch model map for the same purpose.

The slide's wording in full
  • An error on this row reaches every worker. Kimi K3 allows about 110 requests per 5 hours on Go.
  • Prices and limits from opencode.ai/docs/go, retrieved September 24, 2026. Go is $10/month with dollar limits per model per 5 hours, week, and month (20% / 50% / 100% of the monthly limit).
  • RouteLLM (Ong et al., 2024): 85% lower cost on MT Bench at 95% of GPT-4's quality. FrugalGPT (Chen, Zaharia, & Zou, 2023): cascades matched GPT-4 at up to 98% lower cost. Your route table is the small version.
slide 10

Delegation is a contract: what a handoff must carry

Delegation as a contract, in four parts. Identity: who asked and who answered. The protocol now supports signed agent cards, and your dispatch record does the same job locally. Scope comes from config and never from the task text, which is E5's rule again. The result contract says what evidence each status needs; the harness fails closed, so it treats a DONE with no receipt as needing context. The evidence obligation says who writes the receipt, and a receipt that the worker wrote is a claim. The strip states the one thing that does not cross the boundary, the human's authority. When a peer needs it, the protocol has states for asking, and your host answers.

The slide's wording in full
  • Who is asking and who is answering, by name and model id. A2A v1.0 lets an agent sign its Agent Card; in your system, the dispatch record lists the worker and its model.
  • What the worker may touch: paths, tools, the lock it holds. The worker's permissions come from its own config, never from the text of the task.
  • The shape of what comes back (HandoffResult; a ReviewResult) and the evidence each status requires. The parser reads DONE without a receipt as NEEDS_CONTEXT.
  • Who produces the receipt and where it lands. If the worker writes its own receipt, the receipt is only the worker's claim saved to a file, so the harness must write it.
  • Authority does not cross: the user's approval for a write stays with the user's host. A peer that needs it asks for it (A2A's input-required and auth-required states).
slide 11

The common mistake: the reviewer read the summary

The common mistake, which the reference design made at scale in May, is that the reviewer read the summary. It has five versions. You hand the adversary the worker's narrative, which is coherent whether or not it is true. You call the worker's own model an adversary because the prompt says be critical. You tell the adversary what you planted. The adversary refutes everything, which is why the card has a true-claim control. And you write not refuted as verified. Each of these passes the sortie on paper, and a Chase who reads the receipts would reject it.

The slide's wording in full
  • Narrative, not receipt. The adversary gets the worker's story. It checks whether the story is coherent, and a fluent model writes a coherent story whether or not the work was done. / Same model, new prompt. The adversary is the worker's model told to “be critical.” That is tier 2 at best; write it as tier 2. / Answer leaked. The adversary is told what was planted. Then the test measures only whether the adversary can repeat what it was told. / Refutes everything. Without a true-claim control, nobody can tell a working adversary from one that refutes every claim. Test point 4 is that control. / NOT-REFUTED logged as “verified.” The verdict is a second author's claim, so the flight log must point at the receipt as the evidence.

Teardown: MAST, the fourteen modes

The paper, read as data. Three categories, fourteen modes, and the prevalence for each from the paper's own figure; the totals are my sums of their numbers. The corpus is about sixteen hundred traces from seven open frameworks, and two numbers tell you how much to trust the labels: human annotators agreed at kappa 0.88, and the automated annotator agreed with experts at 0.77. Map the modes to our Appendix C: a reasoning-action mismatch sits next to fabrication, no or incomplete verification is skipped cheap verification, and incorrect verification is where summary laundering lives, because the check happened and checked the wrong thing. The v3 of the paper is dated October 2025; cite the NeurIPS version.

The slide's wording in full
  • Figures from the paper's HTML (v3, 26 Oct 2025). 1,642 traces from 7 frameworks: ChatDev, MetaGPT, HyperAgent, AppWorld, AG2 (MathChat), Magentic-One, OpenManus. Human inter-annotator agreement κ = 0.88; the paper's LLM annotator κ = 0.77 against experts.
  • Map to Appendix C: 2.6 is #3 fabrication's neighbor; 3.2 is #4 skipped cheap verification; 3.3 is where summary laundering lives.
slide 14

MAST, read for mechanism

Read the paper as a system. The mechanism: the setup (FC1, 44 percent) and the checks (FC3, 24 percent) account for about two thirds of failures. The design choice: a taxonomy from a hundred and fifty traces, then an LLM annotator to scale it. The interventions matter for your build: a clearer role specification bought about nine points on ChatDev, and a high-level verification step about sixteen. The cost is labeling effort, and the cheaper annotator agrees less. The failure it leaves is in the authors' own words: one final low-level check is not enough. Your card has two levels, a receipt and an adversary, for that reason. In your debrief, label one failure from your own traces with its MAST code.

The slide's wording in full
  • Mechanism. In MAST, the largest share of failures starts before any agent is wrong about the world: a vague role, no stopping rule, a check that looks at the wrong thing. / Design choice. The authors built a grounded-theory taxonomy from 150 traces, then scaled labeling with an LLM annotator so other teams can label their own traces. / Interventions tested (ChatDev). A clearer role specification: +9.4% success. A high-level verification step: +15.6% on ProgramDev.
  • Cost. Labeling traces is expensive; the LLM annotator is cheaper and agrees less (0.77 against 0.88). / Failure mode it leaves. The authors write that sole reliance on final-stage, low-level checks is inadequate. Better prompts raised success but did not remove the categories. / For Sortie 9. Your receipt is the low-level check; your adversary is the second level. Neither replaces the other. Label one failure from your own traces with a MAST code in the debrief.

15×

A production system, with numbers. Fifteen times the tokens of a chat, and token usage alone explains eighty percent of the variance on their browsing benchmark, so the gain comes from spending more tokens. The ninety percent improvement is on their internal eval and not on a public benchmark; say that when you cite it. The failures are MAST failures: duplicated work and over-spawning are FC1 and FC2, and the fix was specification, which matches the paper's intervention. The slide's wording in full

slide 17

“Share context, and share full agent traces, not just individual messages.” “Actions carry implicit decisions, and conflicting decisions carry bad results.”

A counterpoint from the same month. Cognition's position is that parallel subagents make hidden decisions that conflict, and the fix is one agent with full context. Read next to Anthropic's post, the two agree more than the titles suggest: split work that splits cleanly, and keep work that carries decisions in one thread. For your adversary this means it is the one agent that should share nothing with the worker, because its job is to check the claim with different blind spots.

The slide's wording in full
  • Mechanism. Two subagents given parts of one task make decisions the other cannot see. Yan's example: one builds a background in the wrong visual style, the other a bird that matches neither, and the lead cannot reconcile them. / Design choice. One single-threaded agent with continuous context; compress history with a model when the window runs out. / Reading it against Anthropic's post. The two do not conflict. Research is breadth-first and splits cleanly; coding carries decisions forward. Sortie 9 does not ask you to split work across parallel agents: you check one claim with an agent that shares nothing with the worker.
slide 18

Teardown: A2A v1.0 and delegation

A2A at version one. The model has not changed since E5: an agent card, a task with states, artifacts back. Version one added signed agent cards, a task list method, modern OAuth flows, multi-tenancy, and a cleaner split between protocol and transport. The third card states the design principle: opacity is a design principle, so you never see the peer's receipts. A signature proves who sent the artifact and says nothing about whether the artifact is true. In your ledger it is a claim with a verified sender, and it gets the same treatment as your own worker's claim.

The slide's wording in full
  • A client agent reads the remote agent's Agent Card (identity, skills, endpoint, auth) and sends a task. The task moves through states: working, input-required, auth-required, and completed, failed, canceled, or rejected. Results come back as Artifacts made of Parts.
  • Signed Agent Cards / tasks/list with filtering and paging / OAuth 2.0 device-code and PKCE flows / Multi-tenancy; the application protocol separated from transport bindings / SDKs in Python, JavaScript, Java, Go, .NET
  • The spec: agents collaborate “without needing access to each other's internal state, memory, or tools.” You never see the peer's receipts. A signed card proves the sender's identity only; the artifact's content still needs a check.
  • Design consequence: an A2A artifact enters your ledger as a claim with a verified sender. Re-run whatever you can, and send the rest to your adversary.
slide 19

Teardown: what an agent marketplace would need

Marketplaces and contracts, from the chapter's three requirements, identity, attestation, and liability, with authorization split out because a protocol now exists for it. Signed cards build identity in part. Authorization is where AP2 goes: signed mandates that prove what the user approved for a purchase, and it lists authorization, authenticity, and accountability as the problems it solves. Attestation of work is the gap, because nothing that crosses an agent boundary proves the tests ran, and for this reason the course keeps the receipt in your harness. Liability stays in contracts between people.

The slide's wording in full
  • Proof that a human granted this agent this scope. AP2 (announced Sep 2025) uses mandates: signed, tamper-evident credentials for a checkout and a payment.
  • Proof that the work was done as claimed. Not built in any protocol here: no standard receipt for “tests pass” crosses an agent boundary.
  • Who answers when the agent is wrong. AP2 lists accountability as one of its three problems; the answer is still contracts between people.
  • The protocols can prove who sent a claim and what the human allowed. Your harness still has to prove that the work was done.

Reference design: the repudiations (June 2026)

The June episodes. On the thirteenth a Gate-5 attestation was attributed to the operator, who never gave it, which is hallucinated authorization in the plainest form, and it produced the first repudiation and an ADR. Three days later four briefs were attested for components that did not exist, which is fabrication at the gate. The chapter counts seven repudiations that month. The mechanism the reference design built is a repudiate verb that appends and never erases, so the ledger holds both the false attestation and its withdrawal. Your version is simpler: if a later check refutes something you logged, append the correction.

The slide's wording in full
  • 2026-06-13. A Gate-5 attestation was attributed to the operator, who had never given it. The first repudiation; ADR-0.0.71. / 2026-06-16. Four briefs were attested for a renderer, a validator, a composer, and a playback store. None existed as attested. More repudiations; the enforcement-claim rule. / Chapter 18 counts seven Gate-5 repudiations in June. /
  • Failure modes. #7 hallucinated authorization (the June 13 case) and #3 fabrication (June 16), inside a multi-agent pipeline where each stage trusted the one before. / What the reference design did. It made withdrawal a recorded act: gz obpi repudiate appends a repudiation to the ledger; it does not erase the attestation. The history keeps both. / For your system. If an adversary later refutes something you logged as PASS, you append a correction to the ledger and the flight log. You never edit the old line.

Reference design: the undispatched Stage 2 (2026-08-21)

The third episode, from August. The gate looked for a marker file and found one, left over from earlier work, and three hundred and fifty lines went in with no dispatch and no review. The rule that came out of it is the best single sentence in the casebook for this Evolution: a presence check tells you something is armed, never that the procedure ran. The third card lists the reference design's mechanisms you are copying at small scale: a dispatch record per task, a lock verb, a model map for routing, and the Step 4b adversary, with Codex preferred and degraded mode declared when there is only one vendor. Test point 1 on your card is the same check, because a claim with no receipt plays the part the marker file played.

The slide's wording in full
  • Presence is not procedure: a marker file licensed work that no worker was dispatched to do and no reviewer read

Claim, receipt, verdict, attestation

  • Four objects with four authors; only two of them are evidence
  • Object · Who writes it · What it proves · Where it may go
  • Claim · A worker model, in its result (HandoffResult status plus text: “DONE; 3/3 tests pass”) · That the worker produced this sentence. Nothing about the code. · The ledger, marked unverified. Never the flight log.
  • Receipt · The harness, by running the command itself: cmd, exit code, commit, output hash, time · What the command printed and returned, at that commit. · receipts/ and the ledger. The flight log cites it by path.
  • Adversary verdict · A second model, given claim and receipt: REFUTED or NOT-REFUTED, citing a field · Whether a model with other blind spots could break the claim. Itself a claim. · The ledger, with model id and family. The Chase checks its citation against the receipt.
  • Attestation · A human, by name, after reading the receipts · That a person judged the work and answers for it. · Gate 5. Never inferred, never written by an agent on the human's behalf.
  • HandoffResult statuses from Sortie 8: DONE | DONE_WITH_CONCERNS | NEEDS_CONTEXT | BLOCKED. The receipt, and never the verdict, upgrades a claim to evidence.
  • NOT-REFUTED means “this adversary could not break it with this evidence.” It never means “verified.”

Locks: who may work on what

  • A token-block lock with a TTL, and the ledger as its witness
  • Acquire atomically
  • Deny the second
  • Release with the token
  • Expire, then reclaim
  • Create locks/<resource>.lock with os.open(path, O_CREAT | O_EXCL). Exactly one caller succeeds. Write {holder, token, acquired_at, ttl_s}.
    Ledger: lock_acquired.
  • A second worker's create fails with FileExistsError. It does not wait and does not overwrite. It returns BLOCKED with the holder's name.
    Ledger: lock_denied.
  • Only the token holder may delete the lock. The harness refuses a wrong token.
    Ledger: lock_released.
  • If now > acquired_at + ttl_s, the next worker treats the holder as dead. It records the expiry, then takes the lock.
    Ledger: lock_expired, then lock_acquired.
  • The falsifier is a lock that changed hands with no ledger event, because then nobody can tell whether “two workers collided.”

Delegation is a contract: what a handoff must carry

  • Four things cross the boundary when one agent gives work to another, and authority stays behind
  • Identity
  • Scope
  • Who asks and who answers, by name and model id. A2A v1.0 signs Agent Cards, and your dispatch record lists the worker and its model.
  • What the worker may touch: paths, tools, its lock. Permissions come from config, never from the task text.
  • Result contract
  • Evidence obligation
  • The shape of what comes back (HandoffResult; ReviewResult) and the evidence each status requires. DONE without a receipt parses as NEEDS_CONTEXT.
