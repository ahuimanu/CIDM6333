slide 03

The constraint: recurrence is a queue

Read the highlighted row against the recurrent row. Self-attention spends a constant number of sequential steps per layer and keeps a constant path length between any two words, which is why it trains fast on GPUs and why distant words can affect each other. The price is n squared times d of compute per layer. The paper argues that the price is acceptable when n is smaller than d, and in 2017 a sentence of about 30 tokens against a width of 512 met that condition. The condition no longer holds. A context window of a million tokens puts n far past d, and the n squared term becomes the cost Evolution 6 asks you to budget.

The slide's wording in full
  • What the authors faced / A recurrent network reads token 1, then token 2, then token 3. Each step waits for the one before it, so training cannot spread a sentence across a GPU. / The authors let every position attend to every other position at once. This change cuts the sequential chain from O(n) to O(1), and in exchange the compute per layer grows with n².
slide 04

Figure 1, redrawn: two stacks and a loop

Figure 1 of the paper is redrawn here. The left stack, the encoder, reads the source sentence, and the right stack, the decoder, writes the target one token per pass. Each stack repeats one layer six times. The paper's figure draws no arrow from the top of the decoder back to its bottom, yet section 3 says the model is auto-regressive, consuming the symbols it has already generated as input for the next. The dashed arrow on the slide draws that return path, and it is the inner loop of Figure 01, Two Loops. Figure 01b on the course site steps through every box with the paper's own words.

slide 05

Scaled dot-product attention: one line, three choices

The mechanism is one line of math. In self-attention the queries, keys, and values are three projections of the same input. The dot product of a query with every key gives a score, softmax turns the scores into weights that sum to one, and the weights mix the values into the output for that query. The paper makes two choices inside that line, and each has a reason. It divides the scores by the square root of d_k because large dot products push softmax into regions where the gradients are too small to train on. In the decoder it sets the scores for later positions to minus infinity before softmax, so each position predicts from earlier positions only. The grid on the right is computed from random vectors, so it shows the arithmetic of the weights and nothing about language. In Figure 01b you can switch scaling off and raise d_k, and the rows then collapse onto one key, which is the saturation the paper describes.

slide 06

Eight heads, and word order written in waves

Two more pieces complete the mechanism. Multi-head attention runs eight smaller attentions side by side, each 64 wide, so the total work equals one 512-wide head, and each head can learn a different pattern of attention. Table 3 row A tests the count: one head loses 0.9 BLEU against the base model, and 32 heads also score lower. Second, attention by itself has no notion of word order, because the weighted sum treats the positions as a set. The paper therefore adds a fixed pattern of sine and cosine waves to each token vector, fast waves in the low dimensions and slow waves in the high ones, so that each position carries a distinct signature. Table 3 row E shows that learned positions scored about the same. Many later models encode position by other methods (rotary embeddings, for example), and the reason for adding a position signal is unchanged.

slide 07

28.4

Table 2 carries the headline result. The big Transformer scores 28.4 BLEU on English to German, more than 2.0 BLEU above every earlier result, ensembles included, and the base model already beats them. The paper reports cost by a method this course reuses. The authors do not claim a measured FLOP count. They multiply training time by the number of GPUs by each GPU's rated throughput, and they print the rated throughputs in a footnote. You can recompute the base figure: 12 hours times 3600 seconds times 8 GPUs times 9.5 teraflops is about 3.3 times ten to the eighteenth. A number with its method printed beside it is what this course calls a receipt. The French result shows a table-text mismatch. Table 2 says 41.8 and the text of section 6.1 says 41.0, and when a table and its text disagree, cite the table and record the discrepancy beside it, as the slide does. Figure 01c on the course site plots BLEU against cost on a log scale.

slide 08

Evidence 2: one change against a fixed base

Table 3 is an evaluation design, and it is the part of the paper this course reuses most directly. Each row changes one thing against the same base model, and every score comes from the development set, newstest2013, rather than the test set Table 2 reports on. Read the bars: two layers instead of six cost 2.1 BLEU, dropout turned off costs 1.2, one head costs 0.9, and the big model gains a little. Learned positions change almost nothing. That is the shape of the delta gate you build in Evolution 10: a fixed baseline, one change at a time, a held-out set, and a threshold you commit to before you look at the result. The table reports one number per row and no measure of run-to-run noise, so before you trust a row, decide which of these deltas you would accept from a single run.

  • Evidence 2: one change at a time, measured against a fixed base model
slide 09

The recipe the results depend on

The results in Tables 2 and 3 depend on a training recipe as much as on the architecture. Equation 3 raises the learning rate in a straight line for the first 4,000 warmup steps and then lets it fall with the inverse square root of the step number. The grey line on the slide shows a longer warmup, which reaches a lower peak later. The rest of the recipe sits in section 5: Adam with beta two set to 0.98 rather than the usual 0.999, dropout of 0.1 on every sub-layer output and on the embeddings, and label smoothing of 0.1, which the paper says hurts perplexity while it improves accuracy and BLEU. A reported result is therefore the architecture plus its recipe, and when you compare two models in Evolution 10 you compare two recipes as well.

The slide's wording in full
  • The rest of the recipe / Adam with β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.98, ε = 10⁻⁹. / Dropout 0.1 on every sub-layer output and on the embeddings. / Label smoothing 0.1: it "hurts perplexity, as the model learns to be more unsure, but improves accuracy and BLEU score." / Base: 100,000 steps. Big: 300,000 steps.
slide 10

What chat models kept: the right-hand column

The slide shows the same figure with the encoder faded out. GPT (Radford et al., 2018) kept only the right-hand column: token embeddings with position, masked self-attention, the feed-forward layer with add and norm, and the linear and softmax head over the vocabulary. The prompt and the reply become one sequence, and the model reads all of it and writes the next token on each pass. Two further changes reach your harness. Chat models sample the next token instead of running beam search, so the same prompt can produce different output from run to run, and for this reason one run counts as an anecdote in Evolution 10. Most later models move the normalization step to the input of each sub-layer, a placement that trains more stably at depth.

slide 11

From the paper to your harness

The last slide draws five lines from the paper to this course. The n squared term in Table 1 is the context budget of Evolution 6, because every token you add to the window raises the cost of every later call. The output probabilities at the top of Figure 1 are where the paper stops and your harness starts: the harness reads the reply, gates any tool call the model wrote, feeds the result back as the next input, and decides when to halt (Evolutions 1 and 3). Sampling is the reason Evolution 10 has you run the golden set more than once and gate on the delta. Table 3 supplies the shape of that delta gate: one change, a fixed base, a held-out set, and a threshold fixed in advance. The paper's cost estimate supplies the shape of every number in a flight log (Evolution 11): print the method beside the number so your Chase can recompute it.

The slide's wording in full
  • A tool call is text the model wrote. Only your harness runs it, after the gate.
  • A single run cannot separate a real change from run-to-run variation. For this reason, run the golden set several times and gate on the delta.

The constraint: recurrence is a queue

  • Table 1 of the paper, redrawn; n is sequence length, d is the model width
  • Layer type · Complexity / layer · Sequential ops · Max path
  • Self-attention · O(n²·d) · O(1) · O(1)
  • Recurrent · O(n·d²) · O(n) · O(n)
  • Convolutional · O(k·n·d²) · O(1) · O(logₖ n)
  • Self-attention (restricted) · O(r·n·d) · O(1) · O(n/r)
  • What the authors faced
    A recurrent network reads one token per step. Each step waits for the one before it, so training cannot spread a sentence across a GPU.
    The paper's trade: every position attends to every other position at once. Sequential steps O(n) → O(1), and compute per layer grows with n².
  • Self-attention is cheaper than recurrence "when the sequence length n is smaller than the representation dimensionality d, which is most often the case" (§4). In 2017, n was a sentence of about 30 tokens and d was 512.
  • O(n) → O(1)
    sequential steps per layer, recurrent vs. self-attention
  • In 2026, n is your context window, far past d. The n² term is the cost you budget in E6.
04

Figure 1, redrawn: two stacks and a loop

  • Layout, names, and color roles follow the paper. The dashed return arrow is added here (§3 says the model is auto-regressive)
  • Encoder (left)
    Reads the source sentence. Every position attends to every other, in both directions.
  • Decoder (right)
    Writes the translation one token per pass. Its first attention layer is masked, so each position sees only earlier positions.
  • The base model
    N = 6 layers per stack, d_model = 512, 8 heads, about 65M parameters, 12 hours on one machine with 8 P100 GPUs.
  • The loop
    "At each step the model is auto-regressive, consuming the previously generated symbols as additional input when generating the next" (§3).
05

Scaled dot-product attention: one line, three choices

  • §3.2.1 and §3.2.3; the grid is computed from random vectors and shows arithmetic only
  • Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √d_k ) V
  • Q, K, V come from one place
    In self-attention "all of the keys, values and queries come from the same place." Each is a projection of the same input.
  • Divide by √d_k
    Large dot products push softmax into "regions where it has extremely small gradients." The √d_k factor keeps the gradients large enough to train on.
  • Mask the future
    The decoder sets illegal connections to −∞ before softmax, so each position predicts only from earlier positions.
  • Rows are queries, columns are keys, each row sums to 1. The striped cells are the −∞ entries. Figure 01b lets you turn scaling off and raise d_k until each row collapses onto one key.
  • GPT (2018) and its descendants kept this mask unchanged.
06

Eight heads, and word order written in waves

  • Figure 2 (right) redrawn, and the sinusoidal positional encoding of §3.5
  • Multi-head: h = 8 heads, each of width d_k = d_v = 512 / 8 = 64. Total computation matches one full-width head. Table 3 row A: one head scores 0.9 BLEU worse than the best setting, and 32 heads also drop.
  • Position: attention alone ignores word order, so the model adds sine and cosine waves of different wavelengths to each position. Table 3 row E: learned positions gave nearly identical results.
  • Heatmap rows: positions 0–99; columns: dimensions 0–127 of 512; blue −1, amber +1.

The recipe the results depend on

  • Equation 3 (§5.3): warm up linearly, then decay with the inverse square root of the step
  • lrate = d_model⁻⁰·⁵ · min(step⁻⁰·⁵, step · warmup⁻¹·⁵)

    Adam, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.98, ε = 10⁻⁹.
