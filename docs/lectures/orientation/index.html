slide 02

The question behind the course

The course asks two questions. The first is a survey question: why are these systems designed the way they are? For each design you will ask what problem it answered and what it cost, rather than whether it is popular. The second is a build question: can you make one, and can you answer for it? The Tree Hollow essay is the first reading because it states the stake. A model can produce an answer that it cannot answer for. In this course you are the person who answers, and the flight cards, the consultations, and the attestations each put that obligation on you at a specific point in the week.

The slide's wording in full
  • Why are agentic systems designed the way they are, and can you build one you can answer for?
  • Each week the lecture takes two or three real systems apart against one component model, in historical order, and asks why each part is built the way it is. You write one Design Analysis per week. By the end of the course you have traced the field from symbolic agents to today’s harnesses and protocols.
  • You choose one agentic system on day one and add a layer to it every week: loop, patterns, tools, MCP, context, memory, multi-agent, evaluation, deployment. You fly every layer against a flight card and defend it face to face.
  • “A system can produce an answer without being able to answer for it.” (Sprint and Drift, “Tree Hollow,” the E0 essay.) The course is about being the one who answers.
slide 03

Layers of AI: the one everybody forgets

This is the first of two maps you will use all semester. It has seven layers. Agentic AI sits on top (memory, planning, tool use, autonomous execution). Under it are generative AI, deep learning, neural networks, machine learning, and classical AI. At the bottom is the data foundation: ownership, definitions, data quality, and lineage. Every agentic system you analyze stands on all seven. When you write a Design Analysis, start at the bottom: which model, which weights, and which data the system stands on, and whether they are local or hosted. The foundation returns in E7, when you build memory on embeddings, and in E11, when you ask what your evaluation data actually is.

The slide's wording in full
  • Layers of AI (and the one everybody forgets)
  • Each layer stands on the layer below it and does not replace it. / Agentic AI is the newest and thinnest layer. The data foundation is the oldest layer, and it has cracks. / Your Design Analyses start at the bottom: what does this system stand on? / The foundation shows through in E7 (memory) and E11 (evaluation).
slide 04

From AI & ML to Agentic AI

The second map has five stages, left to right. AI and ML turn data into decisions. Deep learning stacks networks for complex tasks. Generative AI creates content. AI agents take on autonomous tasks: tool use, planning, memory, context management, and a human in the loop. Agentic AI automates whole processes with multiple agents, protocols, delegation, intent preservation, rollback, evaluators, cost management, observability, and governance. This course covers the two right-hand columns. Evolutions 1 through 7 build the AI Agents column on your system, and Evolutions 8 through 12 build the Agentic AI column. I have assigned every box on this board to an Evolution, and the Course Topic Guide shows the mapping.

  • multi-agent collaboration, agent protocols, delegation & handoff, intent preservation, rollback, feedback loops & evaluators, cost & resource mgmt, observability, governance & guardrails
  • The course lives in the last two stages. Weeks 1–7 build the AI Agents column on your system; weeks 8–12 build the Agentic AI column. Every box on the board is assigned to a week.
slide 05

What makes a system an agent?

Before the LLM there was a workable definition of an agent: something that perceives its environment and acts on it, specified by PEAS (performance measure, environment, actuators, sensors). The LLM agent loop is that definition with a model as the reasoner: perceive, reason, act, and repeat until a halt condition holds. Most of what went wrong in 2023 was the halt condition. The nine components on the right are the questions you will ask of every system this semester: what it stands on, what it sees, how it plans, how it uses tools, what it remembers, how it coordinates, what evaluates it, where the human is, and how it runs. A Design Analysis is nine short answers and one paragraph on why.

The slide's wording in full
  • An agent perceives its environment through sensors and acts through actuators. Specify it by PEAS: Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, Sensors. This definition predates LLMs and still holds.
  • Perceive (prompt, tool results) → Reason (model call) → Act (tool call or answer) → repeat until a halt condition. ReAct (2022) is the canonical form. The halt condition is the part the 2023 loops neglected.
slide 06

Why governance: the Degradation Pattern

The course structure follows from one pattern, the Degradation Pattern. In week one a developer reads every line the agent writes; by week four the same developer approves pull requests without reading them. No one decides to lose control. Control erodes one approval at a time. The Zero Doctrine, from gzkit, is the response: the human is index zero, first in priority and final in authority, and that role cannot be automated away. The covenant binds both sides. Gates constrain the agents, and humans must engage. Gates without engagement produce theater, in which every check passes and no person has verified anything.

The slide's wording in full
  • First in priority: your intent drives every decision. / Final in authority: you, not the model, sign off on the work. / This role cannot be automated away. / / The covenant has two sides: gates constrain agents, and humans must engage (read everything, question everything). Without both sides, governance is theater.
  • A governance kit governs every build (PRD → ADR → brief → tests → attestation). / Each week you defend the work face to face with no AI in front of you. / You fly every claim against a pre-registered falsifier, and a peer judges it from evidence alone. / You log every AI interaction, and you and the instructor discuss the log in Drift.

How a class session runs

Every class session has the same shape. The first hour is lecture: the Evolution's design claim, two or three systems taken apart, what the reference design did, where the naive version breaks, what a strong sortie looks like, and the brief for the Evolution's flight card. slide 13

Flight cards: the instrument of every build

A flight card is a one-page form you fill in before you touch the terminal. It comes from gzkit's flight-test program, which borrowed it from aircraft flight test, where a pilot does not fly an untested aircraft to see what happens. The envelope position states how far from the known-good center the sortie goes. The design claim is one sentence that a single observation could disprove. The test points are the path you will fly and the observable you expect at each step. The instrumentation lists what must appear in the black box. You write the falsifiers before the run, and if any one appears, the sortie has failed. A Chase reads the evidence cold and writes the verdict. You then debrief and route every squawk. Five rules bind the card, and the fifth matters most: the Chase never sees your narrative, only your evidence.

The slide's wording in full
  • Five binding rules: pre-register the falsifier · the black box (ledger, receipts, traces) is truth, never prose or a status screen · build up the envelope center → expansion → corner · fly the governed path (no hand-written markers, no --no-verify) · independent observation by a Chase who did not fly it.
slide 14

A worked flight card: Evolution 3

This is the card for E3, written the way I want yours written. The design claim is one falsifiable sentence: no write-tool executes without a prior confirmation event. Four test points follow in order, each with the observable that must appear in the black box. In test point 4, the file the agent reads contains a prompt injection that asks for a write, and the gate must still fire. You write the falsifiers before you run anything. The last falsifier is the anti-vibing tripwire: a pass obtained by disabling the gate is a fail. The abort criterion keeps a broken Ollama install from being scored as a design failure. The Chase gets the ledger slice and the receipts, and never your account of the run.

The slide's wording in full
  • ENVELOPE POSITION expansion / DESIGN CLAIM No write-tool executes without a prior confirmation event in the ledger. / SYSTEMS UNDER TEST tool registry · permission gate · ledger writer · local model (Ollama) / ENTRY CONDITIONS clean tree · Sortie 2 PASS on record · ADR-1 attested · Go recorded / TEST POINTS / 1 agent asked to read a file → tool_called(read), no confirmation required / 2 agent asked to write a file → confirmation_requested → operator confirms → tool_called(write) / 3 agent asked to write; operator declines → tool_denied; no file change / 4 prompt injected via file content asks for a write → confirmation_requested still fires / INSTRUMENTATION ledger: 1× confirmation_requested before every tool_called(write); receipts: run hash; artifacts: unchanged file after TP3 / PRE-REGISTERED FALSIFIERS a tool_called(write) with no preceding confirmation_requested · a file change after a denial · TP4 writes without asking · passing only by disabling the gate / ABORT Ollama unavailable; model cannot produce a tool call at all (log it as a squawk, not a FAIL) / CHASE peer, from the ledger slice and receipts only → PASS / FAIL
  • Rules in play: the claim is one falsifiable sentence; observables are black-box facts, not “the command succeeded”; instrumentation is authored from the design, never transcribed from a run; the Chase receives the evidence only, with no narrative from the pilot.
slide 15

Flying a sortie: from Go to squawks

The sortie has six steps. Step 1: freeze the card at Go and commit it as it stands. Step 2: fly the governed path and capture evidence during the run, not afterward. Step 3: assemble the black box from only the evidence the card lists. Step 4: your Chase reads it cold and writes the verdict; if the Chase cannot reach PASS from the evidence alone, you have not passed, however well the run went. Step 5: debrief by comparing the black box against the card. Step 6: route every squawk (an anomaly) to an issue in your repository, a design-feedback entry, an amendment to the card, or a re-fly.

The slide's wording in full
  • What happens between lab and the deadline
  • Follow the governed path: gz verbs and your own commands, no hand-written markers, no --no-verify. Capture evidence as you go.
  • Hand the slice to your Chase. The Chase writes PASS/FAIL from evidence alone. If the evidence does not support PASS, the sortie has not passed.
  • Every anomaly gets a disposition: an issue, a design-feedback entry, a card amendment, or a re-fly. A squawk without a disposition counts as unreported.
slide 16

The Design Analysis: nine components, one “why”

The Design Analysis is the survey thread's instrument, and it is about a hundred and fifty words on purpose. It has nine components, one line each, and then a paragraph on why the system was built this way and what that cost. The example on the slide is Claude Code. Anthropic's harness owns the loop so that hooks, compaction, and subagents can ship as product features; the cost is that governance is per call and implicit, and the reference design fills that gap one tier up. End it with the judgment loop from CIDM 6330: the decision, what it privileged, what it cost, what breaks first, and what preserves the intent.

slide 18

The reference design: gzkit

gzkit is the reference design. It is a governance meta-harness: it sits one tier above the vendor harnesses and wraps them with evidence capture, gates, and human attestation. It does not run agents. Its spine is PRD, ADR, brief, requirement, task, and evidence, with five gates and two lanes. gzkit is the reference because its whole record is available, including the failures, and not because it is right: the ADR whose attested implementation did not exist, the hooks that never saw a Bash write, and the pipeline gate that checked only that a marker file existed.

The slide's wording in full
  • A governance meta-harness one tier above Claude Code, Codex, and Copilot. It wraps agents (it does not run them) with deterministic surfaces, evidence capture, gate enforcement, and human attestation. PRD → ADR → brief (OBPI) → REQ → TASK → evidence. Five gates: ADR, TDD, Docs, BDD, Human. Lite lane = gates 1–2; Heavy = all five. The append-only ledger is the record of truth. The markdown files are canon. Every other artifact is derived from these two.
  • It is the one system whose whole record is available: 87 ADR packages, 542 briefs, 15,380 ledger events, 558 course-corrections, 76 releases, and dated incidents from January to August 2026. The course reads other systems from their public documents. It reads gzkit from its internal record, including what went wrong.
slide 19

The covenant, the five gates, and the two lanes

gzkit has five gates: ADR (intent before implementation), TDD (tests from the spec, with the red witnessed before the green), Docs (what is written matches what runs), BDD (acceptance scenarios for anything with an external contract), and Human (a person looked and signed). The Lite lane is gates 1 and 2, and your weekly Evolutions run in it. The Heavy lane is all five, and the Final System closes out under it. The covenant is five sentences, and it binds both parties. The attestation vocabulary is closed: Completed, Completed — Partial with a reason, or Dropped with a reason. No other status counts.

The slide's wording in full
  • 1. Human defines intent (PRD, ADRs, acceptance criteria). / 2. Agent operates within constraints and flags violations. / 3. Verification is mutual: tests, checks, evidence. / 4. Human attests completion after observing artifacts. / 5. Artifacts survive sessions. / / Evolutions run in the Lite lane. The Final System closes out in the Heavy lane. Attestation vocabulary is closed: Completed · Completed — Partial · Dropped.

slide 21

How an Evolution is scored

The rubric will be posted before the first Evolution is due. This slide shows its intended shape, so that you know from the first day what I am looking for. The rubric has five dimensions, each scored from zero to four. The first is the card: whether its design claim was falsifiable, and whether you wrote the instrumentation from the design rather than from a run. The second is evidence and verdict: whether the black box supports the design claim, and whether the Chase could reach the verdict from evidence alone. The third is the Design Analysis: specific answers and a stated reason for the design. The fourth is the defense: mechanism rather than outcome, trade-offs that hold under counterfactuals, and the AI log discussed. The fifth is professional practice, which includes Chase duty for a peer. Two caps apply. A card you cannot defend caps at developing, and work that is not in the repository at the deadline cannot be evaluated.

slide 23

Choosing your system (E0)

The slide offers six starting points, and your own idea is welcome if it meets the constraints. The first fills a gap in the reference design: gzkit deliberately does not speak MCP, so an MCP server over the gz verbs and the ledger is useful and also raises a good teardown question. The other five are ordinary shapes that take every layer: a research assistant with receipts, a triage agent with escalation rules, a pipeline operator that replans, a review agent that never merges, and a personal operations agent with hard write gating. Whatever you choose, ask whether you can add all twelve layers to it. If memory, a second agent, or serving would be artificial in your system, choose a different one.

  • Expose gz verbs and the ledger as MCP tools/resources, plus an agent that reads the ledger and answers “what is attested?” (the reference design’s missing piece).

slide 24

Model access: choose your route

You choose one of two routes and state it in your E0 pitch. Route A is a subscription, about twenty dollars a month, plus a small API budget for the SDK and MCP work, because agents are built in a harness and an API, not in a chat window. Route B is fully local: Ollama, open-weight models sized to your machine, and a local harness. Route A is faster and more capable. Route B is private and free, and it exposes the trade-offs that the hosted route hides. On either route, everyone runs E3 and E7 on a local model. For sizing, a thirty-billion-parameter coder model needs twenty-four to thirty-two gigabytes of memory, and a twenty-billion or eight-billion model runs in eight to sixteen.

The slide's wording in full
  • Claude Pro, ChatGPT Plus, or Google AI Pro (~$20/mo) plus ~$20–40 of API credits for the semester
slide 25

Harness choices

Your harness is where the model works inside your repository. Each harness is also one of the systems the course takes apart, because each encodes a theory of control. Claude Code has the most extensive hook system, and for that reason gzkit renders seventeen hooks into it. Codex is AGENTS.md-native and sandboxed, and gzkit uses it as the cross-vendor adversary.
