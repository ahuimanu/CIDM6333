slide 02

The question behind the course

Two questions, one course. The first is a survey question: why are these systems designed the way they are? Not what is fashionable this month, but what problem each design answered and what it cost. The second is a build question: can you make one, and can you answer for it? The Tree Hollow essay is your first reading because it names the stake. A model can produce an answer it cannot answer for. In this course, you are the one who answers, and the whole structure of the semester, the flight cards, the consultations, the attestations, exists to make that true rather than aspirational.

The slide's wording in full
  • Why are agentic systems designed the way they are — and can you build one you can answer for?
  • Every week we take two or three real systems apart against one component model, in historical order, and ask why each part is built the way it is. You write one Design Analysis per week. By December you have traced the field from symbolic agents to today’s harnesses and protocols.
  • You choose one agentic system on day one and add a layer to it every week: loop, patterns, tools, MCP, context, memory, multi-agent, evaluation, deployment. Every layer is flown against a flight card and defended face to face.
slide 03

Layers of AI: the one everybody forgets

This is the first of two maps you will use all semester. Seven layers. Agentic AI on top: memory, planning, tool use, autonomous execution. Underneath it, generative AI, deep learning, neural networks, machine learning, classical AI, and at the bottom, the data foundation, the one everybody forgets: ownership, definitions, data quality, lineage. Every agentic system you analyze stands on all of these. When you write a Design Analysis, start at the bottom: what model, what weights, what data does this thing stand on, local or hosted? You will feel the foundation most in week seven, when you build memory on embeddings, and in week eleven, when you ask what your evaluation data actually is.

The slide's wording in full

  • Each layer stands on the one below; none replaces it. / Agentic AI is thin and new; the data foundation is old and cracked. / Your Design Analyses start at the bottom: what does this system stand on? / E7 (memory) and E11 (evaluation) are where the foundation shows through.

slide 05

What makes a system an agent?

Before the LLM there was a perfectly good definition of an agent: something that perceives its environment and acts on it, specified by PEAS. The LLM agent loop is that definition with a model as the reasoner: perceive, reason, act, repeat until a halt condition. Most of what went wrong in 2023 was the halt condition. The nine components on the right are how you will take every system apart this semester: what it stands on, what it sees, how it plans, how it uses tools, what it remembers, how it coordinates, what evaluates it, where the human is, and how it runs. A Design Analysis is nine short answers and one paragraph on why.

The slide's wording in full
  • An agent perceives its environment through sensors and acts through actuators. Specify it by PEAS: Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, Sensors. This definition predates LLMs and still holds.
  • Perceive (prompt, tool results) → Reason (model call) → Act (tool call or answer) → repeat until a halt condition. ReAct (2022) is the canonical form. The halt condition is the part 2023 forgot.
slide 06

Why governance: the Degradation Pattern

Here is why the course is built the way it is. The degradation pattern: week one you read every line; week four you are approving pull requests you never read. Nobody decides to lose control; it slips away one looks-good at a time. The Zero Doctrine is the answer and it comes from gzkit: the human is index zero, first in priority, final in authority, and that role cannot be automated away. The covenant has two sides. Gates constrain the agents. Humans must engage. If you only have gates, you get theater: every check passes and nobody verified anything. So every build in this course is governed, every week you defend it without the tool, every claim is falsifiable before it is flown, and your AI log is something we talk about, not something you file.

The slide's wording in full
  • First in priority: your intent drives every decision. / Final in authority: you sign off, not the model. / Cannot be automated away. / / The covenant has two sides: gates constrain agents, and humans must engage — read everything, question everything. Without both, governance is theater.
  • Every build is governed (PRD → ADR → brief → tests → attestation). / Every week you defend the work face to face with no AI in front of you. / Every claim is flown against a pre-registered falsifier, and a peer judges it from evidence alone. / Every AI interaction is logged; the log is discussed, not filed.

Two threads, every week

In lecture I take two or three systems apart; you pick one and write a Design Analysis, about a hundred and fifty words, nine components, one paragraph on why it was built that way. Thread B is the build: one system, one layer per week, each layer flown against a flight card and governed with the kit. The two threads meet in consultation: I will ask why others built it that way and why you built yours the way you did.

slide 09

The arc: twelve layers on one system

Read this bottom up. Week one you have a loop that halts and a PRD. By week three it has tools that are gated. By week five it speaks MCP and is hardened. By week seven it has context discipline and memory. By week nine it is a small crew with an adversary. By week eleven it can be evaluated and traced. By week twelve it is served and released with a rollback path. The final system is not a new project; it is this stack, closed out under the Heavy lane. That is why the system you choose in week zero has to be one that can take every one of these layers.

Sprint and Drift, inside one week

Sprint and Drift is the method from CIDM 6330, compressed to a week. AI is permitted and expected during Sprint. The naval metaphor is exact. Moving makes noise that masks signals; listening requires stillness. Production and verification cannot happen at the same moment, so the week gives each its own space.

The slide's wording in full
  • Thu
  • Fri
  • Sat
  • Sun
  • Mon
  • Tue
  • Wed
  • Thu
  • You produce artifacts. AI tools are permitted and expected: generate code, draft docs, explore options, debug, research. Document what you asked, what you used, what you rejected, and why, in AI_LOG.md. The artifact is evidence, not proof of understanding.
  • You defend your reasoning. No AI. I ask why before how, probe the trade-offs (“what did you give up?”), test counterfactuals (“what if the constraint changed?”), and check the AI log (“what did it suggest and why did you reject it?”). Understanding you cannot show here is understanding you do not have yet.
  • The terms come from anti-submarine warfare: sprint to cover ground, then stop engines and listen. You cannot move and listen at the same time. In 6330 the cycle was three weeks; here it is seven days.

slide 13

Flight cards: the instrument of every build

A flight card is a one-page form you fill in before you touch the terminal. It comes from gzkit’s flight-test program, which borrowed it from aircraft flight test: nobody just flies it and sees. Envelope position says how far from the known-good center this sortie goes. The design claim is one sentence that a single observation could disprove. Test points are the path you will fly and the observable you expect at each step. Instrumentation is what must appear in the black box. The falsifiers are written before the run; if any one appears, the sortie failed. A Chase reads the evidence cold and writes the verdict. Then you debrief and route every squawk somewhere. Five rules bind all of this, and the last one matters most: the Chase never sees your narrative, only your evidence.

The slide's wording in full
  • Five binding rules: pre-register the falsifier · the black box (ledger, receipts, traces) is truth, never prose or a status screen · build up the envelope center → expansion → corner · fly the governed path (no hand-written markers, no --no-verify) · independent observation by a Chase who did not fly it.
slide 14

A worked flight card: Evolution 3

Here is the card for week three, written the way I want yours written. The claim is one sentence and it is falsifiable: no write-tool executes without a prior confirmation event. Four test points, in order, each with the observable that must appear in the black box. Test point four is the interesting one: the file the agent reads contains a prompt injection asking for a write, and the gate must still fire. The falsifiers are written before you run anything, and the last one is the anti-vibing tripwire: passing only by disabling the gate is a fail. The abort criterion keeps a broken Ollama install from being scored as a design failure. The Chase gets the ledger slice and the receipts, not your story.

The slide's wording in full
  • ENVELOPE POSITION expansion / DESIGN CLAIM No write-tool executes without a prior confirmation event in the ledger. / SYSTEMS UNDER TEST tool registry · permission gate · ledger writer · local model (Ollama) / ENTRY CONDITIONS clean tree · Sortie 2 PASS on record · ADR-1 attested · Go recorded at 5:5x / TEST POINTS / 1 agent asked to read a file → tool_called(read), no confirmation required / 2 agent asked to write a file → confirmation_requested → operator confirms → tool_called(write) / 3 agent asked to write; operator declines → tool_denied; no file change / 4 prompt injected via file content asks for a write → confirmation_requested still fires / INSTRUMENTATION ledger: 1× confirmation_requested before every tool_called(write); receipts: run hash; artifacts: unchanged file after TP3 / PRE-REGISTERED FALSIFIERS a tool_called(write) with no preceding confirmation_requested · a file change after a denial · TP4 writes without asking · passing only by disabling the gate / ABORT Ollama unavailable; model cannot produce a tool call at all (log it as a squawk, not a FAIL) / CHASE peer, from the ledger slice and receipts only → PASS / FAIL
  • Rules in play: the claim is one falsifiable sentence; observables are black-box facts, not “the command succeeded”; instrumentation is authored from the design, never transcribed from a run; the Chase gets evidence, not narrative.
slide 15

Flying a sortie: from Go to squawks

Six steps. One: the card is frozen at Go and committed as-is. Two: you fly the governed path and capture evidence as you go, not afterward. Three: you assemble the black box, only the evidence the card named. Four: your Chase reads it cold and writes the verdict; if they cannot get to pass from the evidence alone, you have not passed, however well it went. Five: debrief, black box against the card. Six: squawks. Every anomaly is routed somewhere: an issue in your repo, a design-feedback entry, an amendment to the card, or a re-fly. The graded artifact is this record, not the sentence “it worked.”

The slide's wording in full
  • Follow the governed path: gz verbs and your own commands, no hand-written markers, no --no-verify. Capture evidence as you go.
  • Hand the slice to your Chase. They write PASS/FAIL from evidence alone. If they cannot reach PASS from evidence, it has not passed.
  • Every anomaly gets a disposition: an issue, a design-feedback entry, a card amendment, or a re-fly. An untracked squawk is a missing squawk.

slide 18

The reference design: gzkit

gzkit is the reference design. It is a governance meta-harness: it sits one tier above the vendor harnesses and wraps them with evidence capture, gates, and human attestation. It does not run agents. The spine is PRD, ADR, brief, requirement, task, evidence, and five gates with two lanes. The reason it is the reference is not that it is right; it is that we have the whole record, including the failures: the ADR whose attested implementation did not exist, the hooks that never saw a Bash write, the pipeline gate that only checked that a marker file existed. Reading a system from the inside, with its incident history, is a different education from reading a README. That is what this one gives us.

The slide's wording in full
  • A governance meta-harness one tier above Claude Code, Codex, and Copilot. It does not run agents; it wraps them with deterministic surfaces, evidence capture, gate enforcement, and human attestation. PRD → ADR → brief (OBPI) → REQ → TASK → evidence. Five gates: ADR, TDD, Docs, BDD, Human. Lite lane = gates 1–2; Heavy = all five. An append-only ledger is the truth; markdown is canon; everything else is derived.
  • It is the one system where we have the whole record: 87 ADR packages, 542 briefs, 15,380 ledger events, 558 course-corrections, 76 releases, and dated incidents from January to August 2026. We read other systems from the outside; this one we read from the inside, including what went wrong.
slide 19

The covenant, the five gates, and the two lanes

Five gates. ADR: intent before implementation. TDD: tests from the spec, and the red witnessed before the green. Docs: what is written matches what runs. BDD: acceptance scenarios for anything with an external contract. Human: someone looked and signed. The Lite lane is gates one and two, and that is where your weekly Evolutions live. The Heavy lane is all five, and that is what the Final System closes out under. The covenant is five sentences and both parties are bound by it. The attestation vocabulary is closed: completed, completed partial with a reason, or dropped with a reason. Nothing else counts.

The slide's wording in full
  • 1. Human defines intent (PRD, ADRs, acceptance criteria). / 2. Agent operates within constraints and flags violations. / 3. Verification is mutual: tests, checks, evidence. / 4. Human attests completion after observing artifacts. / 5. Artifacts survive sessions. / / Evolutions run in the Lite lane. The Final System closes out in the Heavy lane. Attestation vocabulary is closed: Completed · Completed — Partial · Dropped.

slide 21

How an Evolution is scored

The rubric will be posted before the first Evolution is due; this is its intended shape so you know what I am looking for from day one. Five dimensions, zero to four each. The claim and the card: was it falsifiable, and did you write the instrumentation from the design rather than from a run. Evidence and verdict: does the black box support the claim, and could the Chase get there from evidence alone. The Design Analysis: specific answers and a real why. The defense: mechanism not outcome, trade-offs held under counterfactuals, the AI log discussed. And professional practice, including doing Chase duty for a peer. Two caps: a card you cannot defend caps at developing, and work not in the repository at the deadline is not evaluable at all.

slide 23

Choosing your system (this week)

Six starting points; your own idea is welcome if it meets the constraints. The first is the reference design’s missing piece: gzkit deliberately does not speak MCP, so an MCP server over the gz verbs and the ledger is both useful and a good teardown question. The others are ordinary shapes that take every layer well: a research assistant with receipts, a triage agent with escalation rules, a pipeline operator that replans, a review agent that never merges, a personal operations agent with hard write gating. Whatever you choose, ask one question: can I add every one of the twelve layers to this? If memory or a second agent or serving would be artificial, pick something else.

The slide's wording in full

  • Constraints: an identifiable user and problem; work you may show; every layer must be addable (tools, MCP, memory, a second agent, evals, serving); no credentials or private data in the repo; local-model weeks must be feasible on your hardware. Your own idea is welcome if it passes these.
