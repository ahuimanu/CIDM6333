ADR

↑ top

An ADR is an Architecture Decision Record. It states the intent, the decision, the consequences, and the alternatives, and it is written before the code. The course's ADR-1 (E2) and ADR-2 (E7) are the student's own. gzkit's ADRs are numbered by release (ADR-0.18.0).

Who, when. Michael Nygard published the template in 2011 (title, context, decision, status, consequences). The course adds intent and alternatives, as CIDM 6330 does. The decks cite these gzkit ADRs: 0.0.2 (a standard-library CLI, argparse over click: popularity is not rationale), 0.0.9 (the state doctrine, layers of truth), 0.0.31 (the distribution invariant), 0.0.37 (AGENTS.md is rendered, not hand-edited), 0.0.54 (AGENTS.md is a map, not an encyclopedia), 0.0.71 (the first repudiated attestation), 0.0.73 (the hollow antibody), 0.0.74 (MX mode), 0.18.0 (the subagent pipeline).

Why it is in the course. Gate 1 asks whether the ADR exists. A decision with no record cannot be defended in the Drift.

Read more. https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions

Attack card

↑ top

An attack card is E11's pre-registered adversarial test. It states the objective (which is the falsifier), the failure-mode number it targets, the delivery path, the payload's SHA-256, the guardrail under test, and the expected observable. Attestation

↑ top

An attestation is a human's recorded judgment that a piece of work is Completed, Completed — Partial, or Dropped. It is Gate 5. A later reader can repudiate it if the record shows the work did not exist as attested.

Who, when. The term is gzkit's, and the June 2026 repudiations in the incident casebook are the worked example.

Why it is in the course. Gate 5 is the one gate only a human can pass, so attestation is where the human answers for the work.

Brief (OBPI)

↑ top

A brief is gzkit's unit of human review, and OBPI stands for One Brief Per Item. A brief states the requirements, acceptance criteria, and verification for one piece of work. The requirements are numbered (REQ-1, REQ-2) so that a reviewer can cite the one a change fails.

Chase

The Chase is the independent observer of a sortie. A classmate takes the role and writes the verdict from the evidence alone (the ledger, the receipts, the test output), without relying on the pilot's account.

Controller, worker, reviewer

↑ top

Controller, worker, and reviewer are the three roles in the division of labor the course builds in E8. The controller dispatches one task. The worker (also called the implementer) returns a HandoffResult. The reviewer, which cannot edit, returns a ReviewResult. The controller merges only on PASS. The same division is plan, do, check, and it has appeared under one label or another since FIPA.

Why it is in the course. The course reads the coordination-system Teardowns in terms of these three roles.

Degradation Pattern

↑ top

The Degradation Pattern is the slide in human review that occurs as a model keeps producing acceptable output. Careful reading becomes skimming, skimming becomes approving, and approving becomes absence. The system still runs, and nobody is answering for it. The course describes the slide under three names: cognitive surrender (the human stops judging), mediated observation (the human sees only what the model reports), and governance theater (the gates exist on paper and nothing enforces them).

Who, when. Chapter 3 of the Course Topic Guide and the E0 lecture introduce it.

Why it is in the course. The course's governance apparatus exists to interrupt this slide.

Dispatch record

↑ top

A dispatch record is gzkit's one record per role per task. It says who was dispatched, with what brief, and what came back. If there is no record, the role did not run, whatever the summary says: 'credit is never inferred.' A run with one agent declares itself with --single-driver --reason.

Enforcement locus

↑ top

An enforcement locus is the place in the pipeline where a check runs and can refuse. The course distinguishes three: at the call (a schema or a hook), at the effect (a commit-time diff, git diff --cached), and at the door (release).

Who, when. gzkit moved its write fences from the call to the commit locus after the August 2026 hook incident, in which a hook never saw the write it was guarding.

Why it is in the course. The term tells a reader of a guardrail where to look for the check, because a check that does not sit on the path of the write cannot refuse it.

Falsifier

↑ top

A falsifier is an observation, written on the flight card before the sortie, that would show the design claim to be false. The verdict after the sortie is REFUTED or NOT-REFUTED, and 'it worked' is not a verdict.

Who, when. The term comes from Karl Popper's falsificationism, applied here to engineering design claims.

Why it is in the course. The course scores a design claim by the falsifiers it survived, because a demo can run for reasons the claim did not predict.

Five gates

↑ top

Guardrail

↑ top

A guardrail is a mechanism at an enforcement locus that refuses an action whatever the model has decided (a permission deny, a server-side scope check, a hook, a commit-time diff check). A sentence in a prompt asks the model to refrain and cannot itself refuse, so the course does not count it as a guardrail.

Why it is in the course. E11's attack cards each state the guardrail under test, because the course's test of any safety claim is where the check runs and whether it can say no.

HandoffResult

↑ top

A HandoffResult is the typed claim a worker returns to the controller. It carries a status from a closed set (DONE, DONE_WITH_CONCERNS, NEEDS_CONTEXT, BLOCKED), the files changed, and the evidence (the test command and its exit code). The controller rejects anything that does not parse, and there is no default branch that returns DONE.

Who, when. gzkit ADR-0.18.0 is the source. The field names are the course's own.

Why it is in the course. E8's first rule is that a worker's result is a typed claim, and this is the type.

Hollow test (hollow antibody)

↑ top

A hollow test is a check that cannot fail. Examples are a test that compares the code with itself, an assertion with no input that could make it false, and a gate that looks for a file rather than a result. A live negative control (remove the feature and watch the test go red) is the cure.

Who, when. gzkit's June 2026 audit found that its own hollow-test detector was hollow (ADR-0.0.73).

Why it is in the course. E10's Sortie 10 asks for the RED witness for this reason.

Layers of truth

↑ top

The layers of truth are gzkit's three classes of record. L1 is canon, the files in the repository (intent, ADRs, AGENTS.md). L2 is the ledger, the record of what happened. L3 is derived, anything a model wrote about L1 or L2 (summaries, memory). In precedence, L2 beats L1 and L1 beats L3. The file beats the memory of the file.

Who, when. gzkit ADR-0.0.9 states the doctrine.

Why it is in the course. Figure 14 and the memory Evolution apply the precedence rule: a retrieved record is L3, so it loses to the file it describes.

ReviewResult

↑ top

A ReviewResult is the typed verdict a reviewer returns. It carries PASS, FAIL, or CONCERNS, findings that each cite a location (file:line, test id, diff hunk), and a list of what the reviewer checked. FAIL and CONCERNS need a finding, and the controller rejects a verdict with no citations as malformed.

Who, when. gzkit ADR-0.18.0 is the source. The field names are the course's own.

Why it is in the course. E8's second rule is that a verdict counts only if it cites evidence and its author could not edit the code. This is the type that enforces the citation half of that rule.

Sprint and Drift

↑ top
